# Unsolicited redesign

The breakthrough was realising that "I like this organisation" and "I have a
brief" are different sentences, and the gap between them is where the actual
work is. It's easy to pick something you're fond of and then decorate it ---
nicer type, a warmer palette, a photo grid. I only had something worth
building once I stopped admiring Megalo Print Studio and went looking for
where its real site fails the people who'd actually use it: a physical
Kingston venue that names neither its address nor its hours on the page
people land on first, and a workshop list that's six titles with nothing a
person could act on. Once the redesign had a problem to answer --- put where
and when in the hero, group workshops by what they teach rather than dump
them flat --- every other decision (structure, copy, even which pages to
build) followed from that, instead of from taste.

What this changed about the developer I want to be: I want to be someone who
treats "better" as a claim that has to survive a specific, nameable test, not
a vibe. Writing `spec/crit-2.test.ts` to assert the redesign's actual
promises --- the real address is on the page, every page still links back to
the org it's redesigning, nothing needs a script to work --- made that claim
falsifiable instead of just asserted in prose. It also sharpened where
honesty about gaps beats invented completeness: I know Megalo's own workshop
listings don't carry dates or prices, so I didn't invent any to make the
redesign look finished. A design that admits what it doesn't know is more
trustworthy than one that fakes confidence, and that's a habit I'd rather
carry into code than shed under deadline pressure.
